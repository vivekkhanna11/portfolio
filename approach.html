Approach

How I actually work through a site.

I use roughly the same process whether I'm looking at one intersection or a whole neighbourhood's history. It's not a formula so much as a habit: don't propose anything until you understand what's already there and why.

01

Start with what's already on the ground

Before I sketch anything, I want the base layer right: boundaries, land use, demographics, housing type, and as much historical aerial imagery as I can find. For Forest Hill South that meant going back to 1939. For Bridletowne Circle it meant walking the site in Street View before I ever opened a design tool. You can't fix a plan you haven't actually looked at.

This usually means census and neighbourhood profile data, old fire insurance atlases, aerial photo series, and site photography, plus resident survey data whenever a project has it.

Aerial time-series Demographic profiling Site photography
02

Name the actual conflict

It's easy to say a place "feels unsafe" or "needs more density." I'd rather measure it. That might mean mapping a site through Kevin Lynch's paths, edges, nodes, districts and landmarks, running a SWOT, or pulling hard numbers like crossing distance and posted speed at a specific intersection. Once the problem has a shape, the solution stops being a guess.

This step is really about separating symptoms from causes. A bus stuck in traffic is a symptom. Monofunctional zoning and a six lane arterial are the cause.

Lynch: paths, edges, nodes SWOT Safety exposure metrics
03

Check it against what the city already promised

Most municipalities already have an Official Plan, a Vision Zero target, or a cycling network plan on the books. I like holding the site up against those documents directly, because it turns a design opinion into something closer to an accountability check. If a corridor is supposed to hit zero fatalities and it's currently a high-speed, unprotected crossing, that gap is worth naming plainly.

Where I have it, resident survey data gets folded in here too. People's actual comfort levels and trip purposes tend to say more than my assumptions would.

04

Propose something that can actually get built

I try to avoid all-or-nothing masterplans. Instead I break proposals into tiers: what could go in this year with paint and signal timing, what needs a construction season (curb extensions, protected lanes), and what's a longer horizon rebuild (structured parking, a new internal street grid). A plan someone can start on Monday is more useful than a perfect one nobody funds.

I close most studies with a before and after concept and an honest list of constraints or open questions, because the plan is meant to start a conversation, not end one.

Frameworks I keep coming back to

A few lenses that show up across most of the plates in this portfolio.

Framework

Kevin Lynch Imageability

Paths, edges, districts, nodes, and landmarks. I use this to understand how legible a place is on foot, which often has nothing to do with its formal zoning designation.

Framework

Policy Alignment Scorecards

Comparing what's actually built against Official Plans, Vision Zero commitments, and cycling network plans, so the gap between promise and reality is stated plainly, not implied.

Framework

Community Survey Synthesis

Letting resident-submitted data, comfort levels, trip purpose, satisfaction, actually drive the priorities, instead of guessing who uses a corridor and how.
